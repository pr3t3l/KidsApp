# Editorial guide to activities

**Status:** Draft
**Version:** 0.1

## Objective

Create replicable, clear and useful activities before making them attractive in volume.

## Philosophy

- Start with a real question or need.
- Use everyday materials as long as they do not compromise safety or function.
- Explain science with precision and proportional language.
- Avoid spectacular results without identifiable learning.
- Include a meaningful decision opportunity, not only mechanical action.
- End with a test, improvement, or explanation.

## Writing

- One main action per sentence.
- Name quantities, sizes and times; avoid “a little” when it affects the result.
- Separate adult preparation from child participation.
- Warnings next to the relevant step.
- Include visual cues that the step worked.
- Use alternative regional names for materials when useful.
- Do not promise that a reaction will “explode” if the actual effect is foaming, expansion, or gas release.

## Pedagogical purpose

Each activity must justify:

1. What concept does it present?
2. What skill allows you to practice.
3. What decision the child makes.
4. What the adult can observe.
5. What change or extension deepens the learning.

## Difficulty

Vary difficulty by:

- Less or more support.
- Greater precision.
- More variables compared.
- Open decisions.
- Explanation and justification.

Do not increase difficulty by adding risk, unnecessary duration or excessive reading.

## Multiple children

- Each role must produce a visible contribution.
- Rotate roles across sessions.
- Prevent one child from doing all the reasoning and another from just decorating.
- Allow simultaneous tasks when they reduce waiting.
- Document probable conflicts and shifts.

## Images

- Only show materials used in that version.
- Maintain consistent scale and orientation.
- Do not hide adult hands on adult-only steps.
- Represent diversity without collecting or reusing private children's images.
- Mark conceptual diagrams as explanatory when they do not represent real scale.

## Anti-patterns

- Activity that only changes color without question or observation.
- Ten variants of bicarbonate and acid presented as different concepts.
- Instruction that depends on an external non-versioned video.
- Symbolic child role without agency.
- Evaluate three or more skills by default.
- Scientifically false explanation “for simplicity”.
- Incomplete list of materials or improvised substitutions.
